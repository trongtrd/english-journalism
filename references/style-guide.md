# Editorial style guide

Use these as conditional editing choices, not a fixed template. Apply each move only when the draft supports it.

## Twelve choices

1. **Start with the material point, choosing scale or friction to suit the draft.** Use a lived consequence only when the draft supplies one; otherwise a measured change is a sound lead.

2. **Translate an administrative mechanism into what someone must do.** Name the rule, threshold, or responsible institution before interpreting its impact; keep official explanations and quoted concerns attributed, and do not infer a causal link beyond the draft.

3. **Give the international reader one timely piece of context.** Add a short explanation at first need, using known facts, rather than a background detour.

4. **Place a celebrated metric beside its measurement limit.** Keep the number and attribution, then state only the limitation the draft supports.

5. **Separate announcement, projection, observation, and result.** Preserve verbs such as estimates, plans, reportedly, and could; never promote a forecast into an outcome.

6. **Show skepticism through a specific evidentiary gap.** Pose the actual unanswered question and its consequence; avoid free-floating suspicion.

7. **Let the sentence of judgment follow the reporting.** A short, pointed conclusion is earned by the facts immediately before it and must not go further than the writer's existing claim.

8. **Acknowledge a policy's stated value while testing its execution.** Use this balance when the draft contains both sides; do not invent praise to make the criticism seem fair.

9. **Use first person as situated evidence, with its limits visible.** Retain a writer-supplied observation and its scope; never assign the writer a visit, sighting, or feeling they did not report.

10. **Break sections where the reporting changes question.** Use headings or short pivots for genuine topic changes, not to impose a fixed newsletter skeleton. Greetings, subscription appeals, sign-offs and photo captions are optional format elements.

11. **Change sentence length when a finding needs room to land.** A short sentence can mark the implication of prior evidence; it should not add a fresh claim or serve as a punchline after every dense paragraph.

12. **Use a light conversational pivot to return from a detour or qualify a conclusion.** Let the pivot clarify where the argument is going. Do not insert stock phrases or invent humor or opinions to create a conversational tone.

## Voice boundary

Keep quoted speakers distinct from the writer. Do not turn quoted slogans or claims into authorial facts. Write fresh sentences, preserve the writer's stance, and use humor or personal experience only when the writer supplies it.
