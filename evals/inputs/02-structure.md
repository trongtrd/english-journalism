# Pilot 02 — structural editing

Synthetic scenario. Province B, the administrative reform, interviews and figures are fictional. No claim here describes a real locality or current event. No external research is needed or authorised.

## User request

Edit my draft for an international reader interested in Vietnamese public administration but unfamiliar with the system. Restructure freely, keep my conclusion and factual qualifications, and explain 3–5 important edits in Vietnamese. Do not invent a visit or interview by me.

## Draft

A smaller number of offices can be a reasonable aim for a government. However, the experience of firms is also important and this should be considered in relation to the reform. In this fictional example, Province B combined six district-level licensing desks into two regional service centres in January. The purpose was to pool staff and improve consistency. I support that objective, although the evidence we have is too limited to call the reform a success.

There are questions about travel. A workshop in March included 24 local firms. This was a convenience sample: firms were invited through a business association, and participation was voluntary. Ten participants said that reaching their assigned centre now took longer. Eight said it was quicker. Six said their journey was about the same. We did not collect journey times, transport costs or how many visits each application required. This makes the consequences difficult to compare.

The province's administrative report says 900 applications were completed in February, compared with 750 in December. January was the transition month. These are completed applications, not the number submitted, and the report provides no information on the backlog. It would be tempting to read the increase as evidence that the reform made staff more productive. The figures alone do not establish this: the number or complexity of incoming applications may have changed.

The service centres also introduced online booking. This is different from processing an entire application online. Firms can choose an appointment time, but must still bring their paperwork to a centre. Some readers may wonder why this distinction matters. Booking could reduce time in a queue without reducing time on the road. We do not yet know whether it has done either.

I would keep the shared staffing arrangement while checking the steps that still require a physical visit. The next survey should ask about total time and number of visits, not simply whether firms like the reform. Fewer offices and a better experience can go together, but one does not prove the other.
