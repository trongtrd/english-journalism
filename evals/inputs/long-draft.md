# New long-form test — entirely fictional

Province L, its training scheme, firms, report and interviews below are invented for editing evaluation. They are not reporting about a real place. Do not browse or add external facts.

## Request

Edit the draft for an English newsletter about Vietnam's policy and economy, aimed at interested international non-specialists. You may substantially restructure it. Keep my policy position, material facts, attribution and uncertainty. Aim for 330–450 English words excluding teaching notes, without padding. Return the English revision and 3–5 concise Vietnamese explanations containing actual before/after excerpts. Include verification points only for real unresolved choices. Do not invent an interview or observation by me.

## Draft

Training policy needs to consider participation as well as the quality of courses. In this fictional example, Province L launched a six-month voucher pilot in April. Registered small manufacturers could recover half of an approved course fee, up to 2 million dong per worker. This was reimbursement after completion, rather than a discount when enrolling. Officials said the pilot was intended to help firms upgrade workers' skills. I support retaining it for a second, limited round, but not extending it across the province yet.

There was also an administrative result which the provincial department highlighted. By the end of September, 180 vouchers had been issued and 120 workers had completed a course. The department's report gave these as two separate totals; it did not provide a linked record showing which voucher holders had completed. Neither total was a count of firms. We cannot use the two numbers to calculate a completion rate. This is worth making clear because participation figures are often the most visible part of a program.

An independent local business association spoke with 14 participating employers in August. This was a convenience sample recruited from its members, not a representative survey of small manufacturers. Nine said releasing workers for weekday classes was difficult. Five did not report that difficulty. The association did not ask how many hours of production were lost, whether shifts could be swapped, or whether firms hired temporary cover. Employers' answers describe a scheduling problem, but they do not put a cost on it.

It is important to support training, and continuing the pilot seems reasonable to me. At the same time, a voucher paying part of a course fee leaves other parts of the decision with the employer. Firms had to pay the full fee first. Reimbursement could be requested only after a worker completed the course and submitted the required attendance certificate. The department did not publish the time it took to process these requests. We do not know whether reimbursement delays discouraged participation, and I would not claim that they did.

Two approved providers offered evening classes from August. Their enrollment figures were combined with those of daytime providers in the report. It is therefore too early to conclude that evening classes solved the scheduling problem. The association's interviews also covered only firms that had joined the pilot; they tell us nothing directly about why other eligible firms stayed out.

For the next round I would keep the existing reimbursement level, collect linked enrollment and completion records, and ask participating and nonparticipating firms about scheduling and upfront payment. I would compare evening and daytime options before recommending a larger rollout. The useful question is whether firms can use the support, not just how many vouchers were issued. My support for another limited round does not mean the pilot has demonstrated an improvement in productivity: the report contains no productivity measure.
