## English revision

# A training voucher firms still have to pay for first

A training voucher can cover part of a course fee, but a small manufacturer still has to find the money and release a worker from the production schedule. Those are the practical questions facing Province L's six-month pilot, launched in April. Registered small manufacturers could recover half the cost of an approved course, up to 2 million dong per worker. They paid the full fee first and could request reimbursement only after the worker completed the course and submitted the required attendance certificate.

Officials said the scheme was meant to help firms upgrade workers' skills. By the end of September, the provincial department reported 180 vouchers issued and 120 workers who had completed a course. These are separate totals, with no linked record showing which voucher holders completed. Neither figure counts firms, and dividing one by the other would not produce a completion rate. The report also contains no measure of productivity, so the figures cannot show whether training improved it.

The clearest account of a practical obstacle comes from an independent local business association, which spoke with 14 participating employers in August. Nine said it was difficult to release workers for weekday classes; five did not report that difficulty. The employers were recruited from the association's members, so their answers cannot stand for all small manufacturers. The association did not ask about lost production hours, shift swaps or temporary cover. Its interviews identify a scheduling problem for some participants, without putting a cost on it or explaining why eligible firms that did not join stayed out.

Two approved providers began offering evening classes in August. But the department combined their enrollment figures with those of daytime providers, leaving no basis yet to say evening classes solved the scheduling problem. Nor did the department publish how long reimbursement requests took to process. Delays may matter to firms paying upfront, but the available material does not show whether they discouraged participation.

I would retain the pilot for a second, limited round at the same reimbursement level, rather than extend it across the province now. The next round should link enrollment to completion, ask both participating and nonparticipating firms about scheduling and upfront payment, and compare evening with daytime options. Supporting training is sensible. Before a larger rollout, the province needs to know whether firms can use this support and what it achieves, not just how many vouchers it issues.
