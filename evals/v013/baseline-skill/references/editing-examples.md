# Editing examples — constructed, not reporting

These fictional mini-drafts demonstrate editorial moves. Bracketed placeholders stand for facts or citations already present in a user's draft; they are not invitations to fill gaps. None of these examples asserts a current event.

## 1. Policy through a supplied consequence

**Before (fictional draft):** “A proposed rule in [fictional city] would require market stalls to issue digital receipts from [date]. [Local agency] says it would make inspections easier. Several vendors told [named source] they would need new devices and worried about the cost.”

**After:** “For some market vendors in [fictional city], the proposed receipt rule begins with a practical question: how to pay for the device. Several told [named source] they would need new equipment. The proposal would require stalls to issue digital receipts from [date], a change [local agency] says would make inspections easier.”

**Why:** The reported burden leads, then the mechanism and the agency's claim follow. “Proposed” and attribution remain. The cost-focused lead requires both the need for devices and the supplied concern about paying for them.

## 2. A strong number with its limit

**Before (fictional draft):** “[Company] reported [number] product deliveries in [period]. The company is winning over individual customers. The total includes deliveries to a company-owned service, but the split is not available.”

**After:** “[Company] reported [number] product deliveries in [period]. The total includes products sent to its own service, but a breakdown between those deliveries and individual purchases is not available. The total alone cannot show how much demand came from individual buyers.”

**Why:** The revision retains the company-reported total and drops the unsupported claim that individuals are being won over. It states the precise evidentiary gap without implying that individual demand is weak.

## 3. Closures without invented causation

**Before (fictional draft):** “Shops closed after [policy] took effect, so the policy caused the closures. A separate inspection campaign started during the same period.”

**After:** “Some shops closed after [policy] took effect. But an inspection campaign began during the same period, so the timing alone cannot show how many of the closures, if any, the policy caused.”

**Why:** The dates remain, while causation is reduced to what the supplied evidence can support. If the draft contains sourced interviews identifying other pressures, those can be added with attribution; the editor should not supply them.
